The Great Rewiring and the Four Harms: What the Evidence Says, and Where It Is Contested

A widely discussed framework links the phone-based childhood to sleep loss, attention fragmentation, addiction and social deprivation. Here is what it argues, what its critics say, and how we use it to design Pulse.

Dr. Elias Kairos Chen24 October 2026 · 4 min read

Between roughly 2010 and 2015, childhood changed shape. Smartphones with front-facing cameras, always-on social media and high-speed mobile internet arrived in the pockets of millions of young people within a few years. The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt calls this period "the Great Rewiring" of childhood, and his book The Anxious Generation (2024) has become one of the most widely discussed accounts of what it may have done to adolescent mental health.

This article summarises the book's central framework, sets out where other researchers disagree, and explains how we use the framework in designing Pulse, our early-warning concept.

From a play-based to a phone-based childhood

Haidt's core argument is that two trends moved in opposite directions at the same time. In the physical world, children were given steadily less freedom: less unsupervised play, fewer chances to take small risks, less independence. In the online world, they were given far more: smartphones, social media accounts and largely unrestricted access to content designed by adults to capture attention.

He summarises this as young people being overprotected in the real world and underprotected in the virtual world. The result, in his account, is a shift from a play-based childhood, in which young people build social skills, resilience and confidence through face-to-face experience, to a phone-based childhood, in which much of that experience is replaced by time on screens.

The four foundational harms

The book identifies four ways in which a phone-based childhood can harm development. They are useful because each describes a mechanism, not just a quantity of screen time.

Social deprivation. Time online can displace time spent with friends in person. Online contact is not equivalent: it is thinner, more performative and more exposed to comparison.

Sleep deprivation. Phones in bedrooms, notifications and late-night scrolling cut into sleep at the age when the brain most needs it. Poor sleep is closely linked to low mood, irritability and difficulty concentrating.

Attention fragmentation. A constant stream of notifications and short-form content makes it harder to sustain focus on any one thing, including schoolwork, conversation and rest.

Addiction. Many platforms use variable rewards, endless feeds and social validation in ways that make it hard to stop, and that can produce compulsive use and withdrawal-like distress when access is removed.

Four norms for families and schools

Haidt proposes four collective norms: no smartphones before high school (around age 14), no social media before 16, phone-free schools, and far more independent play and responsibility in the real world. He stresses that these work best when families, schools and communities adopt them together, because no parent wants their child to be the only one without a phone.

These norms sit alongside public health guidance. In Singapore, the Ministry of Health advises that children aged 0 to 12 should not be given any access to social media services. The American Psychological Association recommends watching adolescents for signs of problematic use and letting autonomy grow with each young person's maturity.

Where the evidence is contested

The book has strong critics, and families deserve to hear both sides. Reviewing it in Nature, the psychologist Candice Odgers argued that the claim that digital technology is causing an epidemic of mental illness is not supported by the science, and that most studies find small or mixed associations rather than clear causation. Other researchers, including Andrew Przybylski, have questioned the methods used to combine studies and the evidence that delaying phones or social media helps.

Haidt has responded that the evidence, taken together, does point to causation, and that waiting for certainty carries its own costs, which may be larger and harder to reverse.

Our view is that both positions contain something important. Average effects across whole populations can be small while effects for particular young people, exposed to particular content at particular times, are serious. That is exactly why we focus on patterns for an individual, not screen time for everyone.

From framework to signal

The four harms give Pulse a structure grounded in published research. Each harm corresponds to patterns that could, in principle, be sensed on a teenager's own device without reading their messages:

  • Sleep deprivation: sustained use after an agreed bedtime, especially of short-form video.
  • Attention fragmentation: rapid switching between apps, bursts of notifications and long unbroken sessions of endless feeds.
  • Addiction: compulsive re-opening of the same apps, difficulty stopping, and steadily rising time on the same feeds.
  • Social deprivation: screen time displacing time with friends, and changes that suggest withdrawal.

None of these patterns is a diagnosis. Each is a reason to notice, to talk, and, only if a serious pattern persists, to seek support. Before any signal is used with a family, it must be validated against clinical research, with thresholds defined by clinicians.

What families can do now

You do not need a tool to act on this framework. Keep phones out of bedrooms overnight. Notice whether time online is replacing time with friends. Talk about how particular content makes your teenager feel. And give them more freedom and responsibility in the real world, not less.
