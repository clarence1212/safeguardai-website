When the Feed Never Ends: AI-Generated Video and the Adolescent Brain

Generative AI removes the natural limit on how much content exists. For teenagers, whose reward systems mature ahead of self-control, that may change what a feed can do.

Dr. Elias Kairos Chen24 October 2026 · 3 min read

For most of the history of television, film and even social media, every piece of content was made by someone. That simple fact set a natural limit. There was only so much to watch, and only so many people making it.

Generative AI removes that limit. Video, voices, faces and whole serialised dramas can now be produced at almost no cost, tested against what holds attention and tailored to an individual viewer. For adults, this is a new form of entertainment. For teenagers, whose brains are still developing the systems that govern reward and self-control, it may be something more consequential.

A feed with no natural end

Short-form video already relies on recommendation systems that learn what keeps each viewer watching. Until now, those systems could only choose from what people had made. When the content itself can be generated to order, the system no longer has to find the most compelling video. It can make it.

This changes three things at once. The supply of content becomes effectively unlimited. Its precision increases, because content can be shaped to an individual's responses. And its pace can be tuned continuously, so that the next clip always arrives before attention wanders.

Why adolescence matters

Adolescence is a period of rapid change in the brain's reward and emotional systems, while the regions that support planning and self-control continue to mature into early adulthood. Our team's systematic review of neuroimaging studies examines how digital media use relates to this development and to adolescent mental health.

This mismatch is ordinary and healthy in most of life: it is part of how young people learn, take risks and become independent. But it also means that content engineered to be maximally rewarding meets a brain that is especially responsive to reward and still building the capacity to step away.

Real but false

The second risk is truthfulness. AI-generated video can show events that never happened and people saying things they never said. For young people still forming their sense of how the world works, and of how bodies, relationships and success look, convincing fabrication is a particular concern.

The most serious form is already here. In a nationally representative survey of US teenagers aged 13 to 17, Common Sense Media found that nearly half had seen AI-generated explicit material, and one in four of those said it depicted themselves or someone they knew. Synthetic content is not a future problem. It is part of many teenagers' feeds today.

Why existing controls fall short

Most parental controls were designed for a world of websites and apps. They block lists of sites, set time limits or filter known categories. They are less suited to a feed that is generated rather than published, where the same app can deliver calm, creative content one hour and harmful content the next, and where nothing about a video announces that it was made by a machine.

Screen time totals alone also miss what matters. Two hours of drawing tutorials and two hours of appearance-focused AI video are the same number on a dashboard and very different experiences for a young person.

What a better approach looks like

We believe families need to understand patterns of exposure: what kinds of content reach their teenager, how intense it is, when it arrives and whether it is synthetic. That is the idea behind Pulse, our early-warning concept. It would read how apps are rated and used on the teenager's own phone, and, with their agreement, the platform data they choose to share, looking for patterns linked in published research to harm, such as sustained late-night use of endless video feeds.

Crucially, the teenager would hear first. Parents would see agreed summaries, never the content itself. And professional support would be suggested only when a serious pattern persists.

What families can do now

Talk with your teenager about AI-generated content: how to spot it, why it is made and how it can make them feel. Look at their feed together and ask what it recommends. Keep the hour before sleep free of short-form video. And make it easy for them to tell you if they see something that worries them, including content about themselves or their friends.

Pulse is a concept in development and is not yet available.
